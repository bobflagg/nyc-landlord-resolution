Public-records entity resolution

NYC Landlord Resolution

Precision-first probabilistic resolution of NYC landlord/owner identities from public HPD records — so a single owner's buildings stop scattering across separate “portfolios.”

Runs on the two HPD tables alone (hpd_contacts, hpd_registrations). No knowledge graph, no geocoder.

Why it matters

Knowing which buildings belong to the same landlord is the foundation of nearly every data-driven housing investigation in New York — for journalists tracing a negligent operator across the boroughs, tenant organizers finding others stuck with the same landlord, and watchdog agencies targeting the worst actors. Group the buildings correctly and a pattern becomes visible across the whole portfolio; get it wrong and accountability slips through the cracks.

JustFix's Who Owns What is the gold standard for this task. It links landlords by shared names and addresses, so its portfolios are best read as networks of who registers where — often operational networks — and two things can go wrong around them:

False splits

One owner, many portfolios

A business-address typo, a second office, or a per-building shell LLC defeats the match, and an owner's own buildings scatter across unrelated groups.

→ fixed here, by probabilistic record linkage

A second question

A shared address can mean three things

A shared address says buildings register from the same place; what that means depends on the place. An owner's own office points to one owner-operator, a manager's office to a shared operation but not shared ownership, and a shared mailbox or service provider — a registered agent, a law firm — to little at all.

→ answered by the follow-up, NYC Beneficial Owner Resolution

See the false-split problem

Domenico Antonelli, a Queens landlord, has just 10 buildings registered from a handful of offices — but one office, 150-115 POWELLS COVE BOULEVARD, is also typed 150-115 POWELLS COW B, a hyphen is dropped from 146-48, and the rest are different offices, so each spawns its own portfolio. Who Owns What splits him into 9 separate portfolios. Scoring name and address together reunites the variants (9 portfolios become 5 groups); a second pass then joins those groups when their buildings share a company named on the registrations (here his managing agent) and the name is rare, giving one entity. That second pass rests on management evidence rather than a name or an address, and it is the least reliable stage in the labeled sample.

How it measures up

Developed and tuned on a 105-record hand-adjudicated gold set (pairwise precision ≈ 0.996 on that set, an optimistic figure because the clustering threshold was calibrated against it), then measured on the links it would add on top of Who Owns What, using a stratified sample of the candidate frame:

96.7%
of sampled model links judged the same owner (Wilson 92–99%)
64 of 67
sampled links that join separate Who Owns What portfolios were correct (95.5%, Wilson 88–99%)
15 of 18
links through the shared-company second pass, versus 49 of 49 through name and address (small samples, exploratory)

Ownership gets its own question

Reading a shared address as shared ownership is the subject of a follow-up project, NYC Beneficial Owner Resolution (“Watchline”). Where this engine asks “are these the same name?”, that one asks “are these the same beneficial owner?” — pulling in ACRIS deeds and resolved registration identities — while keeping an operational-network layer, with aggregator hubs masked, as its own. It is evaluated on precision only, on a stratified sample judged against primary records by one human annotator and an LLM second reader. Roughly 97% of its deed-held and resolved-identity links were judged the same owner; links through a deed later split into separate LLCs are the weak spot, at roughly 70%; and roughly 98% of pairs sharing a masked aggregator address were correctly kept apart. Recall is not estimated, and there is no head-to-head tally against Who Owns What, which maps co-registration, not ownership. Together, the two projects clean up the operational signal and give ownership its own answer.

These groupings are algorithmic inferences from public records — leads to verify, not determinations of legal ownership.
